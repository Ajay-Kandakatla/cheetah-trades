Week 1Market Language & Measurement

Goal: never get lost in trading vocabulary again — points, equity, ATR, standard deviation.

Points — for an individual stock, 1 point = $1 of price ("NVDA fell 5 points" = fell $5). For an index, points are the index's own units (S&P 500 down 50 points ≈ 1% if it's at 5,000). Points ≠ percent: a 100-point Dow move is tiny; a 100-point S&P move is huge. Always convert to % in your head.
Equity — two meanings: (1) ownership in a company (stocks are "equities"); (2) your account equity = cash + market value of open positions. Minervini's risk rules ("never risk more than 1–2%") refer to account equity.
ATR (Average True Range) — the average size of a stock's daily range over N days (usually 14). A $200 stock with ATR $8 moves ~4% a day. Use it to size positions and place stops outside normal noise (e.g., stop 1.5–2× ATR below entry, if that fits your max loss).
Standard Deviation — a statistical measure of how spread out returns are. ~68% of moves fall within ±1σ, ~95% within ±2σ. Bollinger Bands are just a 20-day average ±2σ — when bands pinch, volatility has contracted (the statistical cousin of a VCP).

Week 2Support, Resistance & Price Action

Goal: understand WHY patterns work — they are pictures of supply being absorbed at resistance.

The core idea (this answers your main question)

Every bullish base — VCP, cup-and-handle, double bottom, flat base — is the same story told in different shapes: a stock hits resistance (a price where trapped buyers and profit-takers want to sell), pulls back, and returns. Each return chews through more of that overhead supply. When sellers are exhausted, even modest demand pushes price through the level — the breakout.

So the question "which pattern grows from resistance?" has a clean answer: the pattern doesn't predict the breakout — the behavior at resistance does. A pattern is likely to resolve upward when:

  • Each pullback is shallower than the last (that's literally what VCP measures) — sellers are losing power;
  • Volume dries up inside the base and near the pivot — nobody left to sell;
  • Price tightens just under resistance instead of falling away from it — demand sits right beneath supply;
  • The stock shows relative strength vs. the index, and the general market is in an uptrend;
  • The base is early in the trend (1st or 2nd base out of a consolidation, not the 4th–5th).

A double bottom, cup-and-handle, or flat base with those traits is a VCP wearing a different costume. Without them, the same shape often fails.

Price Action — reading price and volume directly (candles, swings, ranges) instead of relying on indicators. Core skills: identifying swing highs/lows, trend structure (higher highs/higher lows), range vs. trend, and how price behaves at key levels (rejection wicks, tight closes, wide-range breakout bars on volume).
Resistance / Support — price zones (not exact lines) where supply/demand previously overwhelmed the other side. Old resistance, once broken, often becomes support — which is why breakouts frequently get a "retest."

Week 4Pullbacks, Tennis Ball Action & Short Squeezes

Goal: judge whether a dip is healthy (buyable) or distribution (sellable), and understand squeeze mechanics.

Pullback — a counter-trend dip within an uptrend, typically to a rising moving average (10/21-day EMA, 50-day SMA) or prior breakout level. Healthy pullbacks come on declining volume. The pullback buy is Minervini's lower-risk alternative to chasing extended breakouts.
Tennis Ball Action — Minervini's term: after a breakout, a strong stock pulls back for 2–5 days (up to 2 weeks) and bounces back fast to new highs, like a tennis ball. Weak stocks splat like an egg. Rule of thumb he cites: a healthy leader closes in the upper half of its range in most recent sessions and recovers pullbacks on rising volume. It's a hold/add signal — the institutions are still accumulating.
Short Squeeze — when a heavily shorted stock rises, shorts must buy to cover, fueling more rise. Watch: short interest % of float (>15–20% is high), days-to-cover. Squeezes are violent but end abruptly — they are fuel for a breakout, not a substitute for a sound base.

Week 5Liquidity Concepts (Smart Money / ICT)

Goal: understand liquidity grabs, sweeps, fair value gaps, and supply/demand zones — and how they explain the shakeouts you already see in Minervini setups.

The bridge to what you already know: the "undercut" in a double bottom, the handle shakeout in a cup, the final flush before a VCP pivot — ICT traders call these the same thing: a liquidity sweep. Stop-losses cluster below obvious lows; institutions push price into them to fill large buy orders, then reverse. Different vocabulary, same market mechanics.

Liquidity Sweep — price wicks beyond an obvious high/low (where stops sit), triggers those orders, and snaps back inside the range. Liquidity sits above swing highs (buy stops) and below swing lows (sell stops).
Liquidity Grab — a sweep that leads to a reversal: institutions "grabbed" the liquidity to enter positions. Every grab is a sweep; not every sweep reverses.
Fair Value Gap (FVG) — a 3-candle imbalance: price moved so fast that the 1st candle's high and 3rd candle's low don't overlap, leaving a "gap" of untraded prices. Price often returns to "fill" part of the gap before continuing — a popular entry zone.
Supply Zone / Demand Zone (S/D) — the price areas where a big move originated. A demand zone = the base before a strong rally (unfilled institutional buy orders may remain); a supply zone = the consolidation before a strong drop. Fresher, untested zones are stronger. "S/D trading" = buying at demand, selling/shorting at supply.
Scalp — a very short-term trade (seconds to minutes) capturing small moves with size, dozens of times a day. Opposite end of the spectrum from your swing/position style; know the term, but mixing timeframes is how swing traders sabotage themselves.

Week 6Macro & the Market: Why the Index Leads Your Stocks

Goal: understand the chain Macro → Index → Sectors → Your stock, and what "forward guidance" actually moves.

The causal chain

Macro → Index: interest rates, inflation, employment, and earnings expectations set how much investors will pay for future profits. Higher rates make future earnings worth less today (discounting), compress P/E multiples, and pull money toward bonds — so hawkish surprises knock indexes down; dovish surprises lift them.

Index → Stocks: roughly 50–75% of an individual stock's daily move is explained by the market + its sector (this is "beta"). Index ETFs and funds buy/sell whole baskets at once, so when SPY moves, your stock moves with it regardless of its own merits. This is why Minervini and O'Neil both insist: 3 of 4 stocks follow the general market's direction — trade aggressively only when the index is in a confirmed uptrend.

Practical routine: each weekend check the S&P 500 and Nasdaq vs. their 21/50-day MAs, count distribution days, glance at the economic calendar for CPI / jobs / FOMC dates, and size positions accordingly.

Forward Guidance — the Fed's public signaling about future policy ("rates will stay higher for longer…"). Markets trade on expectations, so guidance moves prices before any actual rate change. Tools: the FOMC "dot plot" and CME FedWatch (rate-cut odds implied by futures).

Week 7Fundamentals: Balance Sheet & Cash Flow

Goal: read the three statements well enough to filter for the fundamentally strong leaders SEPA demands.

Balance Sheet — a snapshot at one date: Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity. For growth-stock screening, check: low/manageable debt (debt-to-equity), rising book equity, and enough current assets to cover current liabilities (current ratio > 1.5).
Cash Flow (Statement) — tracks actual cash over a period in three buckets: operating (cash the business generates — should be positive and growing), investing (capex), financing (debt/share issuance). Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capex. Earnings can be massaged; cash is much harder to fake. A company with rising EPS but negative operating cash flow is a red flag.
How this plugs into SEPA — Minervini's fundamental screen looks for accelerating quarterly EPS and sales growth, expanding margins, and institutional-quality balance sheets. The chart (VCP) tells you when; the statements tell you which stocks deserve a spot on your watchlist.

Week 8Putting It Together: Your Trade Plan

Goal: fuse pattern + price action + liquidity + macro + fundamentals into one repeatable checklist.

Pre-trade checklist

  • Market: index above rising 21/50-day MA? No FOMC/CPI landmine within 1–2 days of entry?
  • Stock: Stage 2 uptrend (above rising 150/200-day), RS line near highs, sound fundamentals (EPS + sales accelerating, positive FCF)?
  • Pattern: recognizable base (VCP / cup-handle / double bottom / flat base), contractions tightening, volume drying up, pivot defined?
  • Liquidity read: did the base already shake out below an obvious low (undercut / sweep)? Bases that have flushed are safer than ones with untouched equal lows beneath.
  • Risk: stop placement (below pivot day low or 1.5–2× ATR), position sized so a stop-out costs ≤1% of equity, reward:risk ≥ 3:1.
  • Post-entry: watch for tennis ball action on the first pullback; sell into weakness if it splats.
